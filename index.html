The Leverage Question: Inside the AI Debt Boom

Category: Macroeconomics & Financial Systems
Written By: Arpit Zachariah
Published on the 3rd of October, 2026
"Debt becomes leverage when borrowed capital creates more value than it costs."

Who is Leopold Aschenbrenner?

Leopold Aschenbrenner is a German-born former OpenAI researcher who became a hedge fund founder. Born to physician parents, he graduated from Columbia in 2021 at age 19 as valedictorian, with a degree in economics and mathematics-statistics. He joined OpenAI's Superalignment team in 2023 and was let go the following year. OpenAI cited a security leak. His version is that he was ousted for circulating a memo arguing the company's protections against model-weight theft were inadequate.

How Situational Awareness came about

  • The essay: Two months after leaving OpenAI, he self-published Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead. It combined scaling-law extrapolation, geopolitics, and AI-lab security into a forecast of AGI around 2027, followed by a possible intelligence explosion.
  • The thesis: AGI would need an unprecedented buildout of semiconductors, data centers, energy grids, and memory hardware. The fund bet on those bottlenecks rather than on the model labs themselves.
  • The launch: The fund is backed by Patrick and John Collison, Daniel Gross, and Nat Friedman. WSJ reports it started with a few hundred million dollars, and that he had no professional investing experience.

The rise and the crash

  • By mid-2026, WSJ reported over $20 billion in assets and investors including Jane Street.
  • At its peak in July 2026, it managed about $45 billion, but it then suffered roughly $35 billion in losses that month.
  • Bloomberg reported the fund nearly imploded under a cascade of margin calls, then completed a $400 million private investment days later.

Some of the performance numbers come from investor letters and anonymous sources, and different outlets report different figures. They are reported claims, not audited results. Since the crash is recent, details are still emerging.

How big is the AI bubble?

There is no agreed size for the "AI bubble". The closest measures are how much value sits in a few AI-linked stocks and how much is being spent on infrastructure.

Scale

  • One data aggregator puts the combined market cap of all listed "AI" stocks at $55.57 trillion, but that classification is very broad, so it is not the bubble's size.
  • Nvidia is the anchor at roughly $5.5 trillion, followed by Apple, Alphabet, Microsoft, and Amazon. The five biggest companies make up about 30% of the S&P 500's market cap.
  • Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Oracle committed roughly $660-690 billion of capex for 2026, nearly double 2025. Nvidia captures a large share of that as chip revenue.

Why it worries people

  • Spending versus revenue: OpenAI's and Anthropic's combined revenues are still a fraction of the infrastructure being built for them.
  • Cash flow: Alphabet's cash flow turned negative in Q2, and Microsoft is down 21% this year despite spending over $190 billion. Markets have started punishing capex.
  • Circular financing: Nvidia's $100 billion commitment to OpenAI raised concerns because Nvidia's investment flows back to it as chip purchases.
  • Credit stress: CDS spreads on Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Nvidia, Oracle, and Broadcom have risen sharply, and some AI-linked IPOs have been delayed (a Forbes piece with a bearish framing).
  • Counterpoint: Nvidia earns real profits at 53% net margins, and the hyperscalers mostly fund spending from cash flow, unlike dot-com firms. The consensus view is bubble-like conditions in specific segments, not the whole market.

What Situational Awareness signals

This is analysis, not an established consensus. The fund was the bull thesis turned into a leveraged trade. It bet on the physical bottlenecks of AGI: chips, power, and data centers. Its rise showed how much capital would chase that story, with a reported $20 billion in assets and over 1,000% returns since inception.

Its collapse then showed the fragility. The fund nearly imploded under a cascade of margin calls, and it went from a peak of about $45 billion to $35 billion in losses in July 2026. The thesis may be right about the technology while the trade is still fragile. Concentration plus leverage can wipe out a correct long-term view in days. Its quick return to dealmaking also suggests private capital was still flowing, which fits the "bubble in segments" view.

Who owns whom?

I could not find evidence that Meta holds Alphabet stock or that Alphabet holds Tesla stock. The real cross-ownership is concentrated in the AI supply chain, and there is a second, indirect channel.

1. Round-tripping through AI labs (the main channel)

Per Motley Fool's analysis, OpenAI buys server access from CoreWeave while owning a piece of it. CoreWeave also serves Microsoft, which buys hardware from Nvidia. Nvidia sells to OpenAI and holds an equity stake in it. Anthropic buys compute from Microsoft, which holds a stake alongside Nvidia.

The risk is that some of the revenue is the company's own money returning, so demand looks stronger than outside demand alone would justify.

2. Common ownership through funds

Index funds hold all seven stocks. A single S&P 500 ETF holds a slice of each, and the seven together are roughly 30% of the index. Investors in those funds own all of them at once, so wealth moves between the stocks without any company buying another's shares. The fund managers (Vanguard, BlackRock, State Street) are the usual holders to check in filings.

3. Bonds

Hyperscalers increasingly borrow to fund data centers. Pensions, insurers, and funds buy that debt, and credit-default-swap spreads on several of these names have risen sharply, per Forbes. Stock shocks can then spread through credit. I have not verified which companies hold each other's bonds.

To trace it yourself: check each company's 13F and 10-K filings ("equity investments" and "related parties"), the top institutional holders, and recent S-1 and 8-K filings for the AI labs.

How the buildout is funded

The funding stack

Capex means buying chips, building data centers, and securing power. The money comes from three layers, in this order:

  • Operating cash flow: Goldman projects about $778 billion of cash flow against about $750 billion of 2026 capex. On paper that is roughly break-even, so there is almost no cushion.
  • Debt: Goldman expects debt issuance to cover about one-third of capex this year. FactSet estimates incremental debt rose from 9% of capex in fiscal 2024 to 32% by mid-2026.
  • Equity: the smallest piece. Oracle, the most stretched, planned to raise $45-50 billion across debt, equity-linked, and common equity.

Spending also hides outside the bond numbers. Alphabet's filing showed over $100 billion of debt plus $85.2 billion of data-center lease payments not yet started. Meta used a $27 billion private-credit joint venture with Blue Owl for its Louisiana campus, and has issued bonds through single-project special-purpose vehicles.

How pensions, insurers, and funds buy the debt

  1. A company hires banks to underwrite a bond.
  2. Banks sell it to institutional investors. Meta's $30 billion deal drew a $125 billion order book.
  3. Buyers tend to be pensions, insurers, and bond funds. They hold long-dated, high-rated bonds to match decades-long obligations like pensions and annuities, which is why a 100-year Alphabet bond could find buyers (general explanation).
  4. Each deal pays a "spread", the extra yield over Treasuries. Reuters found most 2026 hyperscaler bonds now trade at higher yields than at issuance, and new-issue concessions rose from about 2 basis points to 12. Demand is cooling.

Ratings range from Microsoft's AAA to Oracle's BBB. Rating differences explain why the risk is uneven.

How CDS works

A credit default swap is insurance on a bond. The buyer pays a yearly premium (the spread), and the seller pays out if the company defaults. A rising spread means the market sees more risk. Oracle's five-year CDS more than tripled after its September 2025 deal (MUFG), and Alphabet's hit a record in July 2026. Many buyers are not betting on default: a pension holding a big Meta bond can buy CDS to hedge it. Because spreads are visible daily, CDS has become a real-time AI-risk gauge.

Where hedge funds fit

My sources do not detail hedge fund positions, so this is the general mechanism:

  • Credit trading: they buy new bonds that price cheaply, or trade the gap between a bond's spread and its CDS.
  • Hedging or shorting: buying CDS is a cheap way to bet on stress.
  • Leveraged equity: funds borrow from prime brokers to own chip, power, and data-center stocks. This is the Situational Awareness model, and the leverage is why a drop produced margin calls.
  • Liquidity: they provide trading volume. In a panic they also sell fast, which amplifies moves.

So the loop is: hyperscalers borrow, institutions fund the debt, and hedge funds add leverage on the equity. A shock in one layer transmits through the others.

Bubbles compared

The chart ("How to Spot a Bubble", Raising InvestorIQ, citing Dr. Jean-Paul Rodrigue, Hofstra University) shows the shared pattern: stealth, awareness, mania, then blow-off. It is a stylized model fitted after the fact, so it cannot tell you where we are now. A "bull trap" and a healthy dip look the same in real time.

My read: telecom is the best template for the physical buildout, dotcom for valuations, 1929 for leverage, and bitcoin for sentiment. AI blends all four. Bubble-like conditions look real in parts of the market, but the best-capitalized companies are in much stronger positions than in the earlier episodes. Capex-driven selloffs, the July crash, and the first selloffs like DeepSeek match the chart's early warning phases, but that does not tell us the peak is behind or ahead of us. This is analysis, not a prediction.

What they invest, what they get, and who holds the debt

Figures vary by source and by what is counted, so treat these as ranges.

What they are investing

  • 2026 capex: the five hyperscalers are on track for roughly $780 billion in 2026, up from $416 billion in 2025. Other estimates run closer to $660-725 billion.
  • Next few years: UBS projects about $4.1 trillion from 2026 to 2028.

What they are getting

  • Cloud revenue: AWS about $150 billion annualized, Google Cloud $80 billion, Azure AI $37 billion: roughly $267 billion, but much of it is not AI, so it overstates the AI payoff.
  • Contracted demand: the three big clouds hold a combined backlog of about $1.7 trillion.
  • AI labs: Anthropic reported a $30 billion run rate by April 2026, though a rival disputes about $8 billion of it over accounting. OpenAI ended 2025 near $20 billion in revenue and is projected to lose about $14 billion in 2026.
  • The gap: Morgan Stanley estimates the buildout needs about $650 billion of new annual revenue for a 10% return, against $50-150 billion of AI-attributable revenue today. Directly attributable returns are well below spending, though contracted backlog is growing fast.

How much debt, and who owns it

  • Bonds in 2026: the five issued about $220 billion through August. Reuters estimated the combined USD debt footprint at over $360 billion, more than doubling in about ten months.
  • Off-balance-sheet: roughly $65 billion more, such as Meta's $27 billion venture with Blue Owl. The FT says more than $1 trillion of new debt is expected over the next few years.
  • Core holders: a Chicago Booth researcher estimates about $520 billion of senior unsecured hyperscaler bonds sit with bond funds, insurers, pension funds, and similar institutions. A North Carolina pension official described yields near 5% as attractive.
  • Index funds: hyperscalers are about 8% of the investment-grade index, so anyone in a bond fund or ETF owns some.
  • Private credit: in the Meta deal, Blue Owl owns 80% and Meta 20%, with PIMCO among the lenders. New York and Pennsylvania state pension plans invested in Blue Owl's digital infrastructure fund. Apollo and Blackstone created a vehicle to raise $35 billion for Anthropic's compute, serviced from lease payments.

The risk is not that Alphabet or Microsoft cannot pay. It is the weaker links: Oracle, the AI labs, and special-purpose vehicles where the exposure is hard to see. Ordinary savers can end up exposed through pensions and insurance without knowing it.

Total debt, government debt, and ownership

  • Hyperscaler debt: over $360 billion, plus about $65 billion off-balance-sheet; Alphabet alone disclosed $85.2 billion of data-center lease payments not yet started. I do not have a reliable combined figure for the other Mag 7 members (Apple, Nvidia, Tesla).
  • US government debt: federal debt passed $40 trillion on August 18, 2026 and was about $40.2 trillion in late September, growing by roughly $15 billion a day according to one tracker. The hyperscaler total is roughly 1% of that.

Did they fund the government, or the reverse?

Neither, directly. Nothing I found shows the hyperscalers financing the Treasury, or the government financing them. Big tech does hold large cash balances, often partly in Treasuries, but I have not verified current amounts. The real link is competition for the same buyers: pensions, insurers, and foreign investors buy both Treasuries and corporate bonds. The IIF notes government bond yields in the US, Japan, France, and the UK are at decade highs, so a flood of corporate supply meets a rising cost of capital. That is context, not proven cause and effect. The one government-to-chip-industry funding path is the CHIPS Act, which the administration converted into a 9.9% Intel stake.

Government ownership

  • Hyperscalers: no federal equity found in any of them. CSIS says the government has not taken a stake in Nvidia or AMD.
  • Intel: the government holds about 10%, now worth around $44 billion.
  • Nvidia and AMD: they agreed to give the government a 15% cut of some China chip sales. That is a revenue share, not ownership.
  • Indirect exposure: public pension funds and index funds own these shares, so taxpayers hold some indirectly. I cannot quantify that.

Share of world debt

Global debt hit a record $365.5 trillion in June 2026 (IIF). The hyperscaler total is about 0.1% of it, while the US government alone is roughly 11%. The AI debt is tiny globally. It matters because of its growth rate, its concentration in a few borrowers, and the weak links like Oracle and the private-credit vehicles. Government debt is the bigger systemic issue.
